# Summary: What education is needed in the Post-LLM era?

Brian Jang explains why his family decided to send their child to a Waldorf-style alternative school rather than a nearby regular elementary school. The post is not framed as a universal argument that alternative education is the answer. It is a parent’s reflection on what kind of environment may matter more in an AI-shaped future.

The decision is driven by two concerns. First, the author worries that smartphones give children fast, easy dopamine and weaken their interest in slower activities such as reading, making things, and long-form effort. Second, he wants to protect his child’s curiosity and will to learn from the pressures of private tutoring and early acceleration.

The post connects this personal decision to broader AI-era signals: Jensen Huang’s comments that old human skills such as storytelling, journalism, art, design, and communication still matter; Daniela Amodei’s reported emphasis on communication, critical thinking, and the humanities; and a 2011 New York Times article about Silicon Valley tech parents choosing a screen-free Waldorf school.

Waldorf education is described as a model that develops thinking, feeling, and willing together. Students make their own epoch books, learn through rhythm and art, work with wood, knitting, clay, and farming, and avoid screens in the early years. The author interprets this as education for humans who can originate meaning, judge signal from noise, and create the kind of data AI cannot generate by itself.

The conclusion is deliberately non-dogmatic. Montessori, Waldorf, and project/community-based schools differ substantially, and the right choice depends on the child and the family. The larger question is not “what should children memorize before AI automates it?” but “how do we help them learn, discern, create, and relate as humans?”
